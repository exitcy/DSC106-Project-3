About This Visualization

Motivation

The central question is how global near-surface temperatures change by 2100 under different greenhouse-gas pathways. That question matters because the same climate model can produce sharply different futures depending on emissions choices, and those differences are hard to judge from a single static number. CMIP6 is appropriate here: it provides publicly archived, scenario-based simulations of near-surface air temperature (tas) that can be compared on a common baseline.

Visual encoding decisions

Position along a shared year axis and a shared temperature-anomaly axis carries the quantitative comparison. Line marks keep the temporal trajectory readable. Scenario identity uses a blue–amber–red palette (low to high emissions) so pathway differences stay distinguishable without inventing a second quantitative channel. A horizontal baseline at 0 °C anchors anomalies to the 1995–2014 reference. Alternatives considered included a dense global grid map (strong for geography, weaker for precise year-to-year comparison) and end-century bars alone (clear ranking, but they hide when pathways diverge). The slider, focus control, and paired comparison are there to surface timing and magnitude of divergence that a static multi-line chart only hints at.

Data transformations

Source: CMIP6 CanESM5 member r1i1p1f1, monthly tas from the public Google Cloud Zarr archive, reduced to annual means and converted from Kelvin to Celsius. Global series are latitude-weighted with the cosine of latitude. Anomalies subtract the CanESM5 historical mean over 1995–2014. Future lines cover 2015–2100 for SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5; the historical line shows 1850–2014 for context. End-century summary bars average 2081–2100. Missing years are dropped rather than filled. The multi-model strip uses each model’s own 1995–2014 baseline for end-century global anomalies. Limitation: the interactive time series is one model (CanESM5), which runs warm relative to the small multi-model set shown below.
